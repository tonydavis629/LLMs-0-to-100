## Where the Name Comes From

**Lewis et al. (2020), "Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks."**

:::columns cols="2" gap="34px"
**The 2020 paper**

- Retriever and generator trained together
- Gradients flow through both, so the retriever learns what the generator needs
+++
**What most systems run today**

- A **frozen retriever and a frozen generator, connected through the prompt**
- No training anywhere
- The retriever finds text, the prompt carries it, and the generator conditions on it
:::

---

:::figure img="images/lewis_kiela.jpg" name="Patrick Lewis and Douwe Kiela" kicker="Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks (Facebook AI, 2020)" alt="Patrick Lewis and Douwe Kiela"
- Named retrieval-augmented generation and built the first version, with retriever and generator trained together
- The version that spread keeps both frozen and joins them **through the prompt**
- Kiela later co-founded Contextual AI to build products on RAG
:::

## RAG Pipeline

<div class="card-grid cols-4">
<div class="card"><h4>1. Ingest</h4><p>Collect documents and split them into <strong>chunks</strong> sized for retrieval (a page, a section, a paragraph).</p></div>
<div class="card"><h4>2. Index</h4><p>Turn every chunk into a <strong>vector</strong> and store the vectors in an index built for similarity search.</p></div>
<div class="card"><h4>3. Retrieve</h4><p>At query time, turn the <strong>question</strong> into a vector the same way and fetch the nearest chunks.</p></div>
<div class="card"><h4>4. Generate</h4><p>Put the retrieved chunks and the question into a prompt. The model answers <strong>from the chunks</strong> and, ideally, cites them.</p></div>
</div>

Steps 1 to 3 are classical information retrieval, which is decades older than the transformer. Step 4 is ordinary generation. The new work is connecting the two. <!-- .element: class="text-lg" style="margin-top: 12px;" -->

## Dense Retrieval

A **bi-encoder** turns the query and each document into one vector. A pair is scored by **cosine similarity**, the cosine of the angle between the two vectors:

$$\mathrm{sim}(q,d) = \frac{\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{d}}{\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{d}\rVert}$$

:::columns cols="2" gap="34px"
**Why the angle**

- A vector's direction says what the text is about. Its length does not
- Paste an article twice and its word-count vector doubles in length, but the topic is unchanged. Dividing by the lengths removes that effect
- The score runs from -1 to 1, and 1 means the same direction
+++
**How the encoder is trained**

- Contrastively, with **the CLIP objective from Module 8** and text on both sides
- Matching pairs are pulled toward the same direction
- "Smeary", "smudged", and "toner rubs off" end up close without sharing a word
:::

The exercise uses cosine similarity for both retrievers. Its encoder is all-MiniLM-L6-v2: 23M parameters, 384 dimensions, runs on a laptop CPU. <!-- .element: class="text-lg" style="margin-top: 10px;" -->

## The Two Retrievers Fail Differently

<div class="card-grid cols-2">
<div class="card"><h4>Sparse wins on exact identifiers</h4><p>Error codes, part numbers, names, API symbols. "E-341" appears in one document, so IDF gives it a very large weight. An embedding puts E-341 and E-350 almost on top of each other.</p></div>
<div class="card"><h4>Dense wins on paraphrase</h4><p>The user writes "my pages come out crumpled" and the article says "creased or wrinkled output." <strong>No content word overlaps</strong>, so sparse scores near zero. The two embeddings are close.</p></div>
</div>

The exercise is built around this contrast. Each method does what its definition of "nearest" says, and each definition has a blind spot. <!-- .element: class="text-lg" style="margin-top: 12px;" -->

## Retrieval in Production

<div class="card-grid cols-3">
<div class="card"><h4>Hybrid search</h4><p>Run <strong>both</strong> retrievers and merge the rankings with reciprocal rank fusion. The merged ranking covers both blind spots.</p></div>
<div class="card"><h4>Rerank the shortlist</h4><p>A <strong>cross-encoder</strong> reads the query and a candidate together in one transformer pass. It is more accurate than comparing two separate vectors and too slow for a whole corpus, so it reranks the top 100 or so.</p></div>
<div class="card"><h4>Approximate the search</h4><p>Exact nearest-neighbor search is linear in corpus size. Approximate indexes such as <strong>HNSW</strong> give up a little recall and run orders of magnitude faster. A vector database is that index plus storage.</p></div>
</div>

With 48 documents, brute force is instant. These pieces matter once the corpus grows. <!-- .element: class="text-lg" style="margin-top: 12px;" -->

## Where the Pipeline Breaks

<div class="card-grid cols-3">
<div class="card warn"><h4>Retrieval miss</h4><p>Retrieval fetches the wrong chunk and the generator answers from it. The generator did its job, but to the user the result <strong>looks like a hallucination</strong>.</p></div>
<div class="card"><h4>Lost in the middle</h4><p>Models use text in the middle of a long context less than text near the start or end (side quest ahead).</p></div>
<div class="card"><h4>Unfaithful generation</h4><p>A fluent answer can contradict the sources sitting in its context. Grounding makes this rarer. It does not eliminate it.</p></div>
</div>

To fix a bad answer you need to know **which half failed**, so RAG evaluation scores retrieval and generation separately. <!-- .element: class="text-lg" style="margin-top: 12px;" -->

## Evaluating RAG

:::columns cols="2" gap="34px"
**Retrieval**

Needs labeled query-document pairs.

- **recall@k**: did the right document make the top k?
- **MRR**: how high did it rank? (mean of 1/rank of the first relevant hit)
- Both are cheap, exact, and automatic. Both are in the exercise
+++
**Generation**

- Is the answer **faithful** to the retrieved text?
- Free text has no exact-match key
- This is Module 9's judgment problem: use an LLM judge and spot-check it with humans
:::

A wrong answer with recall@k = 1.0 is a generation problem. A wrong answer with recall@k = 0 is a retrieval problem. <!-- .element: class="text-lg" style="margin-top: 10px;" -->

## <span class="side-quest-badge">Side Quest</span> Is RAG Dead?

Context windows grew from 4K tokens to millions. Each jump restarts the same argument: **why retrieve at all when you can paste everything in?**

:::columns cols="2" gap="34px"
**The case for pasting**

- No retriever to build and no misses to debug
- The model attends over everything
- Long-context benchmarks keep improving
+++
**The case for retrieving**

- Every pasted token costs money and prefill latency on every call (Module 10)
- Lost in the middle: attention over a million tokens is not uniform
- Many corpora run to billions of tokens
:::

For a single document, pasting wins. For a whole wiki, retrieval wins. Where the crossover falls depends on the application. <!-- .element: class="text-lg" style="margin-top: 10px;" -->

<!-- .slide: id="sq-lost-middle" -->

## <span class="side-quest-badge">Side Quest</span> Lost in the Middle

Liu et al. (2023) moved the document containing the answer to every position in a long context and measured accuracy at each one.

<div class="metric-box">
<p>The curve is a <strong>U</strong>. Accuracy is high when the relevant document sits at the very start or very end of the context and drops in the middle. On some models the middle positions score <strong>worse than having no documents at all</strong>.</p>
</div>

For RAG this means chunk **order matters**: put the best-ranked chunks at the edges of the prompt. It is also why MRR is worth measuring, since it rewards ranking the right document high and not only fetching it. <!-- .element: class="text-lg" -->
